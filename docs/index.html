Which stale pages should we review first? A decision-support study on anonymized FlyRank content data

Karylle Torres · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring · October 18, 2026

Abstract

This study asks which pages in a content library are most worth reviewing first because they appear to be declining. Using an anonymized slice of 30,000 pages, I compared a transparent hand-written rule (pages not updated for 180 or more days that still earn at least 500 impressions in 90 days, ranked by impressions) with a small decision tree. Both were scored with Precision@K on 20% of clients that the tree never saw during training. On those unseen clients, the rule’s top 20 were declining 80% of the time and its top 50 were declining 70% of the time, while the tree reached 55% and 58%, against a base rate of 51%. These are observed, directional results from a single split, offered to help prioritize human review; they do not show why pages decline or that refreshing a page improves it.

Introduction and problem statement

A content team cannot review every page. The decision this work supports is simple: which pages should a person look at first? A page is labeled “declining” when its recent search trend is down. A good ranking puts declining pages near the top of the list, so reviewers spend their limited time where it is most likely to matter.

Data

I used the anonymized starter slice that ships with the FlyRank ML Internship repository: 30,000 pages, one row per pseudonymized content item, with observed search and engagement metrics, content metadata, and freshness fields. It contains no client names, domains, URLs, page titles, keywords, or search queries. Hashed client identifiers were used only to keep clients separate between training and testing, never as model inputs. Impressions are measured over a 90-day window (impressions_90d). Missing or infinite values in the model inputs were replaced with 0. The full hosted release was not used in this version. Overall, 54.2% of the 30,000 pages are labeled declining.

Methodology

Label. A page is declining when its trend direction is “down.”

Baseline (hand rule). A page scores its 90-day impressions if it is both stale (days_since_last_update of at least 180) and visible (impressions_90d of at least 500); otherwise it scores 0.

Model. A decision tree of depth 3, balanced for class sizes, using six inputs: content age, days since last update, 90-day impressions, average position, click-through rate, and word count.

Validation. I held out 20% of clients (6,163 pages) as an unseen test set, so no client’s pages appear in both training and testing. The hand rule has no training step and was scored on the same test pages. The metric is Precision@K: of the K pages ranked highest, the share that are actually declining.

Leakage check. The label is derived from the trend fields, so trend direction and trend percentage were excluded from the inputs. As a demonstration, adding trend percentage made a depth-2 tree reach a Precision@50 of 1.000 when scored on its own training data. That perfect score reflects leakage, not skill, and none of it is used in the results below.

Results

On unseen clients, the hand rule ranked declining pages higher than the small tree at both cutoffs, and both beat the base rate. In an earlier teaching run scored on the training pages themselves, the rule scored 0.90 and 0.68 against the tree’s 0.55 and 0.60. The two runs agree that the rule is strong at the very top of the list, and the tree is close to the base rate everywhere.

Limitations and honest framing

  • These are observed, directional measurements from one random split with one seed. Another split could shift the numbers.
  • Precision@20 rests on only 20 pages (16 correct), so a difference of a few pages moves it noticeably.
  • “Declining” is a simple trend label, not proof of why a page lost visibility. Nothing here shows that refreshing a page causes recovery.
  • This is a small anonymized slice. Results may differ on the full release.
  • The tree was deliberately kept small so it stays readable; a more flexible model might do better.
  • Nothing in this study predicts or explains how Google’s algorithm works.

Ranked recommendations

These are decision-support suggestions for ordering human review, not guarantees.

  1. Review first: pages not updated in 180 or more days that still earn at least 500 impressions in 90 days, ordered by impressions so the most exposure is looked at first. In the unseen-client test, about 8 in 10 of the top 20 and 7 in 10 of the top 50 were declining.
  2. Monitor next: pages that are visible but were updated more recently. Re-check them on a regular schedule.
  3. Low priority for now: pages with little visibility. Revisit them if their impressions rise.
  4. Before relying on this at scale: repeat the comparison on the full release with several random splits.
